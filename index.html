Purpose of the Study

o This study is to explore London’s deep rooted societal dynamics embedded in something as niche as the city’s street names. By analysing these names, we hope to uncover the biases that have shaped the city’s identity over time particularly those tied to the patriarchy, class and colonialism. Our goal is more than just cataloguing theses names but to examine whose history is being erased and how we can shed a light onto how we commemorate a diverse range of figures in our currently urban environments as naming is a form of representation.

Why Do Street Names Matter in London?

o London is a city shaped by centuries of history, and street names are often the most immediate form of "memory" embedded in the urban space. From the legacy of the British Empire to more contemporary debates around who gets honoured in public spaces, London’s streets can reveal power dynamics that have shaped the city's development.

Data Collection

How the Data was Collected

The dataset used in our project was sourced from the Ordnance Survey OS Open Names dataset, downloaded via data.gov.uk. This dataset is a comprehensive, publicly available database containing street names, place names, and postcodes across Great Britain. It includes accurate and up-to-date geographic information, with over 870,000 named roads and associated coordinate data. The data was downloaded in CSV format, which allowed it to be processed using command-line tools. To make the dataset manageable and relevant to the project, it was filtered to include our three selected areas: Streatham, Edmonton, and Newham. This filtering process involved using terminal commands (e.g., grep, csvcut) to search for specific location names and extract only the relevant rows from the dataset. After filtering, the cleaned datasets were imported into Google Sheets, where further organisation manual based on gender took place. At this stage, each street name was examined individually and categorised (e.g., male, female, unisex, or unnamed). After we researched the origin or meaning of each name manually as well. Additionally google maps was used to verify street locations because we wanted accuracy on our selected areas.

What Counts as a Name?

In our project, a “name” refers specifically to the official street name as recorded in the OS Open Names dataset. This includes the main identifying part of a street (e.g., Victoria, King, Baker) along with its full form when necessary (e.g., Victoria Road, King Street). We mainly focused on Proper nouns excluding the suffix (e.g., Road, Street, Avenue). This is because suffixes do not typically carry gender or cultural meaning.

Names were then categorised based on perceived gender association:
o Male (e.g., names historically associated with men)
o Female (e.g., names historically associated with women)
o Unisex (names that could not be clearly assigned)
o Non-personal names (e.g., places or occupations terms)
It is important to note that not all street names refer to people. Many are derived from locations, landmarks, or descriptive terms. Therefore, only names that could reasonably be interpreted as personal or gendered names were included in the gender analysis. This distinction is important because street naming practices often reflect broader cultural and historical values, including patterns of gender representation in public spaces.

Streatham Findings - Anya Patel

Out of the 388 streets in Streatham, only 18.9% were "named" streets. Of these, the majority were male: 16.5% being named after men, compared to just 1.3% after women (Fig. 1), meaning there were roughly 12 times as many male-named streets to female.

Of all gendered street names, 90.1% were male, 7% female, and 2.8% unisex (Fig. 2). This reveals a clear imbalance in who is publically represented, highlighting how urban spaces reflect historical patterns of who gets recognition and who gets excluded.

Of the small number of female street names (five in total, excluding unisex names), two were named by a single landowner, Beriah Drew: one after his wife's maiden name, Elizabeth Prentis (Brown, 2003, pp.15) and another after his daughter, Jane Angles Fisher (Gower, 2012, pp.16). The remaining female names had no documentation of their names having much or any historical significance that I could find. In contrast, many of the male street names were accompied by a historical story (which can be explored by clicking the markers on the map), reinforcing male visibility and existing historical patterns.

Another thing I noticed was that this pattern extended beyond just street names, but in public institutions as well. In Streatham, no schools were found to have had any female names or connotations, compared to the seven named after male figures. This suggests that gender imbalance is not limited to street naming, but is embdedded all throughout the naming of public spaces. These have been plotted as black markers on the map.

Although not the main focus of this project, patterns of racial representation were also evident. Of the male street names identified, only two appeared to be named after people of colour - Namba Roy Close, and Francis Barber Close - compared to at least 19 named after white men. This indicates that inequalities in representation extends much farther beyond gender alone, reflecting greater structures of exclusion within everyday spaces, and shaping whose histories are seen, remembered, and valued..

Edmonton Findings - Levy Nguyen

Looking at the 666 streets analysed in Edmonton, there are roughly 7.5 times more streets named after men than women.

Focusing specifically on gendered street names, The imbalance highlights how urban naming practices continue to reproduce historical patterns of recognition and exclusion, privileging male figures in the public landscape. This disparity can be better understood in relation to the historical development of Upper Edmonton (see Enfield Society). Much of the area’s street layout emerged during the 19th-century expansion of London, when suburban development was closely tied to landownership and industrial growth. Street names from this period often commemorate landowners who were predominantly white, male, and associated with British imperial and Christian traditions. As a result, the naming landscape reflects a historically narrow representation that does not align with the area’s current diversity.

Contemporary Edmonton is shaped by multicultural communities, including significant Nigerian diaspora and Turkish Cypriots populations, yet these identities are largely absent from the street naming system. This contrast underscores how street names can function as cultural artefacts, preserving older power structures while overlooking newer forms of social and cultural contribution.
